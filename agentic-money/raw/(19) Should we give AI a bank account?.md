For a while now, the dominant way to talk about AI has been as a productivity tool, but there is a deeper shift underway. Humans are not only using AI to participate in the economy more efficiently — they are also starting to deploy agents to act inside the economy on their behalf.

We're moving from chatbots to increasingly autonomous actors that can evaluate options, make decisions, execute transactions, and interact with other systems on their own. That is a categorically different kind of change than "better software." Better software speeds up what humans already do. This idea changes where most economic action occurs entirely. It is the beginning of a new economic architecture for the AI age.

Sean Neville — who helped co-found Circle and the USDC stablecoin — is already building for that world. His new company is not a bank that uses AI; it's an AI-native financial system designed for a different kind of customer: agents that may exist for minutes, execute a task, and disappear. The near-term version of this shift is not some science-fiction trading floor full of independent AIs. It is much more mundane, and therefore much more important. At his company, hundreds of agents already handle compliance monitoring, reconciliation, and liquidity management (functions that today require teams of humans logging into dashboards) supervised by a handful of people.

His bet isn't that agents capture a meaningful share of transactions, it's that they eventually become all of them. Every transaction, in his view, will eventually be executed by AI. That sounds like science fiction, but if we can build the right guardrails, agents will be the safest, fastest, and lowest-cost way to move value. Once that's true, it becomes the default.

That framing forces hard questions: How do you verify identity when your "customer" doesn't have a Social Security number? How do you enforce rules on something that can act autonomously? Who is liable when something goes wrong?

If you accept that agents will become trustworthy enough to take over economic transactions, the market potential is massive. We often ask whether AI will help a lawyer draft a contract or help a consumer find a better flight — and while those are real use cases, they still assume the human remains the sole economic agent, and the AI is merely a tool in their hand.

Modern commerce was built for human beings and all of our limitations. We built interfaces for eyes, buttons for fingers, funnels for attention, trust signals for human psychology. Agents don't need any of it. They don't browse, they don't get nudged by UI patterns, they don't abandon checkout flows. They parse data, evaluate constraints, and execute toward objectives. Which means brand, as we currently understand it, may not survive the transition intact. If there's no psychology to appeal to, there's no brand affinity to build. The entire apparatus of modern marketing — attention, aspiration, identity — was designed for a mind. What replaces it when the buyer has none?

In other words, much of the internet economy was designed around human attention. What comes next will increasingly be designed around machine intention.

Every platform shift has a moment where the technology is ahead of the institutions that support it. That is where we are now. The shift from humans using AI to AI acting inside the economy is no longer theoretical. It is already underway in the least visible parts of the system. The question is whether we build the trust infrastructure quickly enough to support it at scale.
