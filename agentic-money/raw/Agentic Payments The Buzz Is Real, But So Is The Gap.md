Agentic payments are getting a lot of buzz. There’s a plethora of conversation about what AI agents might eventually do financially, how autonomous systems could transact and what a future agent-driven economy might look like. But if I’m being honest, the substance has been limited so far. The excitement is real, but the operational maturity simply isn’t there… yet.

Looking at the maturity curve for agentic payments, we’re essentially right at the start. Autonomous agent frameworks are exploding in popularity, with developers experimenting at a scale we haven’t seen before. That experimentation is already driving spikes in tooling usage and early financial exploration around agents.

But it’s important to recognize that most current AI agent applications still operate inside fairly rigid workflows. Coding assistants, legal research tools and customer support routing are all impactful examples, yet they aren’t truly autonomous economic actors. They execute defined processes rather than independently deciding to procure services or allocate budget. Agentic payments become relevant when agents move beyond assisting humans and begin acting on their behalf in more open-ended ways.

Interestingly, the regulatory picture around agentic payments is still evolving; not because regulators are ignoring it, but the current frameworks weren’t built for autonomous agents. As the technology matures and trust catches up, clearer regulatory guardrails will naturally start to take shape.

Programmatic forms of money align naturally with AI agents because they are digital natives. Banking account structures might have to advance further, particularly toward delegated sub-accounts meant specifically for AI agents rather than shared access to human accounts. Know your customer and identity verification is probably the most unresolved layer. Verifying humans is straightforward compared to verifying autonomous agents and linking them to determinable individuals or organizations. Solving that identity layer will be paramount for scaling agentic payments safely and responsibly.

Personally, I don’t think the actual challenge here is the lack of use cases. If anything, the number of use cases for agentic payments is practically limitless. In any environment where software is making decisions, allocating resources or completing tasks on behalf of humans, financial capability will inevitably become a consideration. The actual challenge is trust. Organizations are still determining how much autonomy they are prepared to grant AI agents, particularly when it comes to money.

This is only natural. Trust in models hasn’t kept pace with their capabilities. Even as AI models continue to advance at a rapid rate, most organizations are still trying to figure out at what point they can trust agents to make payments on their own. Payments are a higher-stakes activity because financial errors are perceived as being more harmful than informational errors.

Security considerations underscore the importance of this caution. Trying to retrofit human financial workflows onto AI agents (for example, providing access to credit cards or bank accounts) introduces obvious risks. You can’t fully guarantee sensitive information won’t leak, and that uncertainty makes organizations cautious. What’s needed instead is financial infrastructure built specifically for agents with secure, revocable access models, delegated sub-accounts and policy-driven permissions that allow financial authority without exposing everything.

The encouraging part, however, is that trust usually follows demonstrated capability. As models improve, which they are, consistently, and as early adopters demonstrate that they are safe and successful, trust will grow. That’s typically how new financial infrastructure emerges. At first, the experimentation phase feels uncomfortable, but over time, those proof points reduce perceived risk, and adoption accelerates.

And as AI systems continue to mature, that trust is likely to catch up faster than many people expect.

As agents become more autonomous and less constrained by fixed workflows, payments become the mechanism that permits the agent to act. Financial access allows an agent to go from suggesting action to taking it. Without the ability to transact, even the most sophisticated AI will reach a point where a human needs to intervene to approve, pay or finalize a transaction.

Fast-forward a decade, and this change begins to feel revolutionary. Consider a personal AI that not only notifies you that your flight has been cancelled but also rebooks it, gets you compensation and changes your plans accordingly, all within parameters you have set. Or consider businesses having agents negotiate contracts, manage vendors and coordinate services, with leadership simply reviewing outcome summaries instead of monitoring each step.

In these cases, payments are the mechanism that allows autonomy to exist in practice. When agents can transact within boundaries, the limitation is imagination, not human capacity.

Adoption-wise, fintech companies will almost certainly lead the way. They historically move faster than traditional banks and are more willing to experiment with new infrastructure. Banks will likely watch closely, experiment internally and eventually step in once ecosystems mature.

We’ve seen this pattern repeatedly with financial innovation. Fintech experimentation creates proof of concept, and traditional banks follow once risk profiles become clearer. There are obvious potential applications within traditional banking, including areas like payroll automation, insurance payouts and expense management, but widespread deployment will likely take longer there.

The biggest open question right now isn’t technological capability; it’s who becomes the first adopter at scale. Agentic payments will probably require someone willing to experiment, accept some friction and potentially absorb financial risk while the infrastructure matures.

Those early pioneers often unlock the value that later makes adoption inevitable. The value of innovation is already attractive, ranging from the automation of business operations to quicker procurement and new digital business models. But someone has to move first, and right now it’s still unclear who that will be.

In three to five years, the agentic payment system is likely to be a mature and sophisticated one, with secure financial accounts for the agents, programmable payment flows and marketplaces tailored specifically for machine-to-machine payments. The governance framework may evolve from approval-based to natural language policies that define the financial constraints within which the agents must operate, while still retaining autonomy.

With such a system in place, agents will be able to oversee procurements, track inventories and automatically redirect suppliers, without being under constant human observation. If that trajectory holds, agents could become a new class of internet users. By mere numbers alone, there could be more agents making online transactions than humans.

The agentic payment system is still in its infancy. But what’s forming is the financial layer of autonomy. As the models become better and trust builds, the notion of agents being able to make transactions is no longer a matter of choice but a reality.
