[JPMorgan Chase & Co.](https://www.bloomberg.com/quote/JPM:US) and [Citigroup Inc.](https://www.bloomberg.com/quote/C:US) have long competed for dominance in global payments, processing trillions of dollars worth of flows from corporates moving money across borders. That rivalry is now extending to new payment rails.

As digital money moves closer to the financial mainstream, the two banks are building competing systems, but taking slightly different paths. Citigroup has signaled openness to stablecoins, partnering with crypto exchange [Coinbase Global Inc.](https://www.bloomberg.com/quote/COIN:US) to build payment capabilities, while simultaneously running its tokenized deposit service. JPMorgan has centered its strategy on in-house infrastructure and has been more cautious on stablecoins, pointing to limited wholesale client demand so far.

Still, both are pressing ahead as these new forms of payment gain traction. JPMorgan’s blockchain-based payments platform Kinexys has over $3 trillion in volume, with average daily transactions exceeding $5 billion, while Citigroup’s tokenized deposit service has more than 500 clients enabled and processes $1 billion dollars each day, on average.

These figures pale in comparison to the trillions in overall daily volumes handled by the two banks on traditional systems every day, but point to how they are preparing for a world in which transactions become increasingly tokenized and around the clock, said Umar Farooq, global co-head of JPMorgan Payments, and Shahmir Khaliq, global head of services at Citigroup, in a recent joint interview.

“The real client problem that we were looking to solve is the ability for large multinationals, big banks and broker-dealers and fintechs to be able to move their money around, make payments seamlessly around the world 24/7,” Citigroup’s Khaliq said.

Stablecoins are digital assets typically designed to track the value of US dollars one-for-one and backed by reserves made up of short-term Treasuries and cash. They’ve soared in popularity over the past year, boosted by new rules in the US championed by President Donald Trump. Deposit tokens, instead, are digital representations of deposits that clients hold in bank accounts. They are designed to preserve many of the same features of their analogue counterparts including [Federal Deposit Insurance Corporation](https://www.bloomberg.com/quote/3688Z:US), fractional reserve lending and interest payments.

A key sticking point in ongoing discussions over crypto market structure legislation is whether yield-like rewards on stablecoin balances should be allowed. The issue has sparked fierce regulatory debate and drawn opposition from smaller banks, which fear it could trigger a flight of deposits from the traditional banking system.

While stablecoins don’t pose an immediate threat to large lenders, stablecoin issuers should be regulated more like banks, Farooq said.

“If you’re taking the same sort of risk, you should have the same sort of regs,” he said. Citigroup’s Khaliq agreed.

JPMorgan began developing its blockchain infrastructure more than a decade ago. Today, Kinexys supports blockchain-based deposit accounts, tokens and securities. Late last year the bank started rolling out its deposit token called JPM Coin to institutional clients. Earlier this year, the bank launched JPM on external public chain Base, with plans to add support for the [Canton network](https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2025-10-29/drw-leads-talks-to-raise-500-million-for-canton-token-treasury).

Citigroup started developing its blockchain platform five years ago. Citi Token Services works across five markets today — the United States, the UK, Ireland, Hong Kong and Singapore — enabling global firms to transact at any time or day of the week.

“Our focus has always been on being able to move money around real time, 24/7, across the globe,” Khaliq said. “This new technology actually gives us a leg up in how we execute that strategy.”

At the same time, the bank is open to working with clients who are looking to make cross-border stablecoin payments, Khaliq said. Citigroup is also working on offering crypto custody services, which it expects to go live later this summer.

For JPMorgan’s Farooq, demand for stablecoins has yet to materialize in wholesale payments, but the bank is open to working on it were that to change. Deposit tokens are better suited to large corporate payments as they integrate compliance infrastructure, including sanctions screening, anti-money laundering controls and regulatory reporting, built up by banks over decades, he said. Stablecoin issuers, by contrast, may take what he called a “lightweight” approach to KYC, he added.

On interoperability — how deposit tokens from different banks will ultimately work with one another — both bank leaders described it as a problem actively being worked on via bilateral discussions or through industry-wide initiatives, like recent efforts led by [Swift](https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2025-09-29/swift-to-build-a-blockchain-based-ledger-for-financial-firms), which runs the messaging system used by financial institutions globally to send instructions on transactions.

Both banks are also watching the role artificial intelligence stands to play in reshaping payments. That is especially true when it comes to “agentic commerce,” through which AI agents transact on a users’ behalf, or machine-to-machine payments. Both realms involve stablecoins’ programmable and Internet-native nature having advantages over traditional card rails.

“If I look five years out,” Citigroup’s Khaliq said, “this is a world that is going to change radically, driven by AI and blockchain.”
