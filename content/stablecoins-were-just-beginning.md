# Stablecoins Were Just the Beginning — Sean Neville Interview

**Source:** [Currency of Power podcast](https://www.currencyofpower.co/p/stablecoins-were-just-the-beginning)
**Interviewer:** Marieke Flament
**Guest:** Sean Neville (Circle co-founder; Catena Labs founder)
**Published:** 2026-03-28

## About Sean Neville

Co-founded Circle in 2013 with Jeremy Allaire. Co-invented USDC. CENTRE Consortium (USDC governance) launched 2018, closed 2023 when governance folded back into Circle. Now building Catena Labs (AI-native financial institution).

## On USDC history

- Market cap didn't meaningfully grow until 2020 despite 2018 launch
- Regulatory clarity (GENIUS Act) took 7 years to achieve from founding Circle
- First USDC white paper was "mostly wrong"; second version (taken to market with Coinbase as first major partner) was "mostly right"
- Lesson: build, try, listen, iterate — the path is non-linear

## On stablecoin regulation

**GENIUS Act framework (US):**
- Reserves: US T-bills, cash, highly liquid instruments only
- Stablecoins function as "narrow banks" — separates money-creation from payment functions
- Depositors could access yield (from treasury returns) while the institution takes a margin

## The "singleness of money" problem

Multiple stablecoins each claiming to equal $1 creates fragmentation. If USDC ≠ USDT in liquidity, settlement guarantees, or regulatory backing, the system breaks interoperability. This is the largest obstacle to stablecoin mass adoption — more fundamental than technical infrastructure.

## On Catena Labs

Built for AI agents as the intended customers:
- Cryptographic enforcement replaces human oversight
- AI-native risk management from first principles
- Every financial transaction will eventually be executed by AI — Catena is built for that world

## On what's next

"Within a foreseeable horizon, every financial transaction will be executed by AI." The question is how to build for that world without repeating the gatekeeper mistakes of the early internet.

## On building

Sean's parallel to music composition (he taught at Berklee College of Music): both require listening to all players, connecting multiple domains, and iterating. Specialization is less productive than cross-domain thinking.
